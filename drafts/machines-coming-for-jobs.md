Every automation panic since the Luddites has followed the same shape: a new machine arrives, some loud voices declare the end of work, the machine underperforms the hype in some ways and exceeds it in others, and the economy quietly reorganizes around it. The panic about AI is the latest iteration. What is different this time is worth separating from what is merely familiar.

## What tends to survive

When people say "the machines are coming for jobs," they usually mean the tasks inside jobs. This is the distinction that keeps getting lost. A job is a bundle of tasks — a radiologist reads images but also talks to patients, writes reports, consults with colleagues. Each task has a different automation frontier. When a tool automates the reading of images, the radiologist does not vanish; the job reshapes around the tasks the machine cannot do, which turn out to be the ones that felt like the real job all along: judgment, communication, responsibility.

This is why previous automation waves did not produce permanent mass unemployment. The Luddites were right that the machines destroyed specific livelihoods — they were wrong that humanity would never again find work. What looks like a single transformation from inside one generation is, from a distance, a series of shifts: machines take the repeatable parts of work, humans keep the parts that are hard to specify, and the boundary moves.

## What is genuinely different

The new thing is that what the machines can take now includes parts of jobs we considered uniquely human: writing, coding, analysis, even the drafting of legal documents. The boundary has moved into judgment territory.

And this is where the honest conversation gets uncomfortable. Some of those tasks will not come back. A company that used to pay a writer for forty hours of work may now pay for eight hours of editing AI output. Not because the writer is worse, but because the marginal cost of prose has collapsed. The economist's language — prices, marginal costs, substitution — is keeping up even if the culture's language is not.

## What the panic gets wrong

The panic treats the collapse in the price of a task as if it were the collapse of the person who did it. They are not the same. What actually tends to be reallocated is the *time* of the person. The writer whose draft time is halved can produce more work, or better work, or work that requires the judgment no one has figured out how to automate.

The uncomfortable corollary: this only works if the people whose tasks get automated are in a position to capture the value of the freed time. That is a distributional question, not a technological one. It depends on ownership, on bargaining power, on whether the cost savings flow to the person or to the shareholders. Nothing about the machine determines that. The machine is agnostic; the power balance decides.

> "The machine is agnostic; the power balance decides."

## What we should actually watch

Instead of asking "will AI take jobs," ask more specific questions:

- Who owns the productivity gains when a task gets 10x cheaper? The person doing the work, or the entity that owns the tool?
- What happens to the young, who traditionally learned a craft by doing its grunt work first? If the grunt work is automated, how does the apprenticeship work?
- Which jobs have a thicket of non-automatable tasks around the automatable core, and which jobs are thin?

The answers will differ by industry, by country, by how much regulation and union density exists. There is no single answer to "will the machines take the jobs" because there are a thousand separate negotiations happening, quietly, across the economy, and the outcome of each is a matter of power, not prediction.

## A calmer prediction

Here is a prediction with low confidence but decent reasoning: the next twenty years will not feature a world without work. They will feature a world where work is cheaper, where the cheapness is captured unevenly, and where the skills that survive are the ones hardest to specify — which is a large part of why they were underappreciated in the first place.

The machines will not do everything. They will do the parts of everything we were not paying enough attention to, because we thought the value was in the output when it was actually in the judgment. That is a serious thing to notice. It is not the end of work. It is the end of the idea that work is primarily about producing output rather than exercising judgment. Those have never been the same thing. We are just being forced to admit it in public.
